# ASCII animation on the web: creatures, motion and character

A bird holds its body steady while its wings beat. A shark turns through the water. A jellyfish’s bell contracts before its tentacles follow. **The appeal of the recent ASCII animation examples is the subject coming alive through the marks**: silhouette, weight and movement remain readable even when the image is made from tiny characters.

That is what makes the examples circulating on X more interesting than a terminal-style texture. Marcelo Design X’s October 26 jellyfish composition, for instance, gives a familiar chat interface an underwater setting. The creatures establish the atmosphere while the text and input remain ordinary, readable UI. It is the combination of a recognizable subject and restrained presentation that carries the scene.

## A subject before an effect

The recent posts explore different subjects. Praveen Kumar’s October 26 [ASCII Web Section](https://x.com/praveenisomer/status/2114680944196264294) places pale purple globes and sculptural forms inside an editorial grid. [Another October 26 post](https://x.com/evanlong_me/status/2114784842441465922) discusses jellyfish and cyberpunk imagery, while an [October 25 example](https://x.com/Moric21/status/2114168604212211740) uses an AI prompt for a colored fantasy scene.

Their shared quality is **a scene that survives the restricted visual language**. A jellyfish needs a soft bell and trailing filaments; a bird needs a convincing body, wings and beak. Filling a rectangle with animated punctuation does not automatically create either. Character density, shading and the timing of movement have to describe the object.

The term “ASCII” is also used loosely in this work. Some renderers use printable characters, others add block symbols, Braille dots or dithered pixels. Those choices affect the surface, but the composition still depends on what the viewer can recognize inside it.

These posts show renewed interest, rather than the invention of a technique this week. Kumar’s [earlier brain-wave exploration](https://design-on-x.com/praveenisomer/status/2063221867155206263/), archived in June, already combined ASCII, dither and particles.

## Tools help build the scene; they do not choose it

An image model can generate a picture that resembles character art. A coding assistant can instead help build a renderer that calculates the scene or converts images into a character grid. The first produces an image; the second produces a program whose motion, lighting and characters can be changed directly.

That distinction appeared in [Creative Bloq’s February 2025 coverage](https://www.creativebloq.com/ai/im-obsessed-with-an-openai-o3-generated-image-to-ascii-art-converter) of Mike Bespalov’s Monospace converter, which he said he built with help from a reasoning model. AI assisted the tool’s construction; an explicit conversion process generated the characters.

Today’s repositories offer several starting points. [Asciify](https://github.com/ayangabryl/asciify-engine) converts images and video and supplies character-based interface effects. [ascii.rest](https://github.com/bas3line/ascii) provides animated subjects and generative scenes. [ascii-sharks](https://github.com/stackedlol/ascii-sharks) concentrates on swimming creatures through a canvas-based ocean engine. All three repositories identify MIT licensing for their code; another creator’s artwork still needs its own reuse permission.

They solve different authoring problems. Choose a converter when the source image is the composition. Choose a procedural renderer when the object’s movement needs to be designed. A ready-made scene can be a starting point when its subject already fits the page.

## Give the scene a reason to be there

A marine subject can give a quiet interface a sense of depth. A bird can suggest lightness and movement. A technical object can make a feature card feel engineered. The subject should support what the page is trying to communicate, rather than being chosen only because it looks unusual in characters.

Keep navigation, labels and important data outside the character field, and check the scene at the size readers will actually see. Fine detail that disappears on a phone is not helping the composition. The [broader design-trends article](/blog/ui-design-trends-skeuomorphism-isometric-glass/) examines the same relationship between visual treatment and usable interfaces.

The strongest examples reward a second look: first you see the creature or scene, then you notice how little visual material was needed to make it feel alive. That is the interesting direction in this revival—recognizable subjects with carefully observed motion, expressed through a deliberately limited set of marks.
