# AI-assisted ASCII animation is finding a place on the web

Over the past two days, a series of **ASCII-based animations** has been circulating among web designers: jellyfish drifting through an interface, colorful fantasy scenes and sculptural forms made from characters. With AI helping creators generate scenes and build rendering tools, an old visual technique is finding new life on the web.

The interest is not simply in making a picture out of punctuation. These examples use **live movement** to give characters, creatures and environments a presence within a page. A jellyfish’s bell contracts and its tentacles follow; a bird’s wings move while it travels through a landscape. The text characters become the material through which the scene is rendered.

## From text characters to moving scenes

ASCII art traditionally builds pictures from letters, numbers and punctuation arranged on a grid. Characters have different visual densities: a period leaves most of its cell empty, while a symbol such as `@` fills more of it. Placed together, they can suggest light, shadow and shape.

Animation adds time to that grid. One approach converts successive frames of an image or video into characters. Another draws a scene in code and samples it repeatedly, updating the characters as its subjects move. Color can be retained from the underlying image or assigned to different parts of the scene.

The term **ASCII** is often used loosely in these examples: some also use block characters, Braille symbols or dithering. The common visual idea is a scene expressed through a limited grid of marks, rather than a conventional smooth image.

## Why AI is part of the conversation

The technique predates the current wave. What has changed is how readily someone can experiment with it. An AI image tool can help develop a visual concept; a coding assistant can help create the renderer, animation and controls needed to put that concept on a page.

Those are different outputs. An image that looks like ASCII art is still a static image. A live renderer updates the scene over time, allowing the movement and character conversion to respond to code.

AI-assisted tooling is not entirely new either. In [Creative Bloq’s 2025 coverage](https://www.creativebloq.com/ai/im-obsessed-with-an-openai-o3-generated-image-to-ascii-art-converter), Mike Bespalov described building an image-to-ASCII converter with help from a reasoning model. More recently, [ascii.rest](https://ascii.rest/docs/) has documented ways to use its animated character pieces with coding agents.

The recent posts are a visible burst of experimentation, not proof that every example was made with AI or that AI invented the style.

## From experiments to interfaces

The examples shared over October 25 and 26 explore different directions. [Praveen Kumar’s ASCII Web Section](https://x.com/praveenisomer/status/2114680944196264294) uses sculptural forms within an editorial grid, while [another post](https://x.com/evanlong_me/status/2114784842441465922) discusses jellyfish and cyberpunk imagery. A [colored fantasy scene](https://x.com/Moric21/status/2114168604212211740) shows an AI-prompted approach.

They point to a use beyond terminal nostalgia: an animated character field can provide atmosphere for a landing page, a product introduction or a quiet background behind an interface. The subject still needs to remain legible, and the surrounding text and controls need enough visual separation to be usable.

Tools such as [Asciify](https://github.com/ayangabryl/asciify-engine), [ascii.rest](https://github.com/bas3line/ascii) and [ascii-sharks](https://github.com/stackedlol/ascii-sharks) illustrate different starting points, from converting images and video to rendering animated scenes. The choice depends on whether the source is an existing visual or a scene whose movement needs to be built.

The technique itself is familiar; the current interest comes from applying it to living scenes and interfaces, with AI making more of the experimentation accessible. The result is another visual language for the web—one whose appeal depends as much on movement and composition as on the characters on screen.
