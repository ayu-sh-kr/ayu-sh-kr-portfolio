# AI Agent Cloud Hosting: How Long Can a $5 Sandbox Last?

Another day, another cloud agent launch. On November 3, Agent37 pitched a **$4.76 sandbox** that can stay awake all month against a **$249 Vercel estimate**. The gap is big enough to ask what each figure buys. How much CPU can your agent actually use, what happens when the host runs short of RAM, and can the provider keep charging that little as it grows? [Launch reporting](https://runtimewire.com/article/agent37-says-a-249-vercel-sandbox-should-cost-4-76-a-month)

Say you leave Codex running overnight to fix a build. A hosted task can leave you a patch by morning. Keeping the same browser login tomorrow or a bot listening all weekend calls for a different kind of hosting. That’s where the price claims get harder to compare.

## The machine, the agent and the model

A sandbox gives software a shell, files, a network and sometimes a browser or desktop. The **agent harness** decides which tools to call, sends context to the model and feeds the results back into the next turn. A host can sell the machine alone, install a harness like Claude Code or Codex, or run the whole session as a managed service. The same model can therefore sit behind very different products.

When Cube pauses, it keeps the disk and drops running processes. Superserve says it can resume the whole machine, and Maritime documents process snapshots on its microVM hosts. A paused bot still needs something to wake it. Agent37’s founder says outbound listeners such as a polling Telegram bot must stay awake unless you arrange an external wake path. [Cube pause](https://docs.cube.computer/docs/billing/) · [Superserve lifecycle](https://www.superserve.ai/) · [Maritime sleep](https://maritime.sh/docs/how-it-works) · [Agent37 launch discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50059096)

## How much does the overnight agent actually cost?

Keep one roughly 4 GB workspace for a 730-hour month and compare 40 hours awake with 730 hours awake. Agent37 comes to **$0.60** for the first case ($0.36 of disk plus $4.40 × 40/730) and **$4.76** for the second. Cube moves from **$4.44** to **$12** because the paused workspace has its own rate. Maritime stays at **$2**, with its CPU allocation and wake behavior distinct from an always-awake VPS. DigitalOcean’s comparable Basic Droplet is **$24** when you retain it all month, and the Manus Standard computer is **$30**. These are workspace costs. Disk sizes, CPU priority and isolation differ. [DigitalOcean Droplets](https://www.digitalocean.com/pricing/droplets) · [Manus billing](https://www.manus.im/features/cloud-computer)

![Estimated monthly workspace bills at 40 and 730 awake hours. Maritime $2 and $2, Agent37 $0.60 and $4.76, Cube $4.44 and $12, DigitalOcean $24 and $24, Manus $30 and $30.](/news/assets/ai-agent-cloud-hosting-costs/workspace-prices.svg)

Active-CPU plans answer a different question. Suppose a sandbox is provisioned for **40 hours** with 4 GB of RAM, but uses just **20 core-hours**. Upstash’s Small pay-as-you-go CPU line is **$2.00**. Vercel’s default-region rate gives **$2.56** for CPU and **$3.39** for provisioned memory, or **$5.95** before storage, credits and other fees. At 80 core-hours, those same meters come to **$8.00** and **$13.63**. These are calculated scenarios, with no throughput benchmark behind them. [Upstash rates](https://upstash.com/pricing/box) · [Vercel Sandbox rates](https://vercel.com/docs/sandbox/pricing)

![At 20 active core-hours, Upstash meters $2.00 and Vercel $5.95. At 80 core-hours, Upstash meters $8.00 and Vercel $13.63. Vercel’s totals each include $3.39 of provisioned RAM.](/news/assets/ai-agent-cloud-hosting-costs/active-cpu-prices.svg)

The **$249 Vercel figure checks out** at list rates if two cores run flat out for 730 hours and 4 GB stays provisioned, before storage, credits or fees. Vercel Pro caps a session at 24 hours, so that price represents aggregate use. Agent37’s **$4.76** buys 730 hours awake on shared compute. Need a guest kernel to run Docker inside the sandbox? At Agent37’s published VM multiplier, the same 730 hours and 4 GB of disk come to **$11.36**. Each figure prices a different condition. None measures how quickly a build finishes when neighboring tenants are busy. [Vercel billing and limits](https://vercel.com/docs/sandbox/pricing) · [Agent37 VM rates](https://www.agent37.com/pricing)

## Who supplies the compute and the context?

**Maritime** gives the clearest split. Its Linux boxes and Android phones run on Hetzner in Falkenstein, its Windows desktops run on AWS EC2 in the US, and its dashboard, API and scheduler run on Railway. Neon stores control metadata and S3 stores uploads and templates. **Agent37** uses Hetzner and OVH hosts with gVisor isolation for its low-cost offering, according to launch reporting. **InstaBox** says it owns its bare-metal fleet and has described nodes running out of memory during free-tier abuse. **Upstash** documents AWS us-east-1 for Box. **Cube** names Fly.io as the operator of its cloud machines and filesystems. [Maritime security](https://maritime.sh/security) · [Agent37 reporting](https://runtimewire.com/article/agent37-says-a-249-vercel-sandbox-should-cost-4-76-a-month) · [InstaBox capacity](https://instabox.sh/blog/instabox-pricing) · [Upstash regions](https://upstash.com/pricing/box) · [Cube subprocessors](https://cube.computer/privacy/)

AWS names itself as **Manus’s** strategic cloud provider and describes Manus using Bedrock for model selection. That announcement covers the agent platform and predates the persistent Cloud Computer launch. The public materials reviewed for **Superserve** and **OpenComputer** give product or runtime details without naming their managed compute suppliers. Superserve’s code shows a Firecracker daemon designed for bare metal. OpenComputer’s v1 docs describe KVM and snapshot-based hibernation. Those details identify how the guest runs. The hardware supplier and region are separate facts. [AWS announcement](https://press.aboutamazon.com/aws/2025/12/manus-selects-aws-to-power-general-purpose-ai-agent-serving-millions-globally) · [Manus launch](https://manus.im/uk/blog/manus-cloud-computer) · [Superserve source](https://github.com/superserve-ai/sandbox) · [OpenComputer architecture](https://docs-v1.opencomputer.dev/how-it-works)

The **content** the agent sees usually comes from the repo you connect, files you upload, sites it browses, and tools or services you authorize. A prepared image gives it packages and tools. A persistent disk can add browser cookies, cloned repositories and earlier work. The configured model provider receives whichever context the harness sends in model calls. Keeping a desktop local with Xiryn leaves the workspace on your machine, while external model or tool calls can still send data outside it. [Xiryn data flow](https://github.com/SouravBeraAkaSpeed/xiryn) · [Maritime data flow](https://maritime.sh/security)

## Where Claude, Codex and the clouds overlap

**Claude Code on the web** and **Codex Cloud** already pair a coding agent with a hosted task environment. For a GitHub task that ends in a patch, the built-in experience handles setup and handoff. Cube or InstaBox can run those same agents when you want to return to a machine you control or keep several tools alongside the repository. Agent37 and Maritime go after a different buyer who wants a sandbox per customer or the option of a browser, desktop or phone. On the API side, **Claude Managed Agents** adds a managed agent loop and bills **$0.08 per running session-hour** plus model tokens. That puts it closer to OpenComputer’s managed sessions. OpenAI’s **Agents API** also offers the managed Codex harness in public beta, with OpenAI-hosted or self-hosted execution environments. [Claude Code cloud](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/12618689-use-claude-code-in-the-cloud) · [Codex Cloud](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001545-using-codex-cloud) · [Claude Managed Agents pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing) · [OpenAI Agents API](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/agents-api/overview)

The closer infrastructure competitors include **E2B**, **Daytona** and **Fly.io Sprites**. They make it possible to compare isolation, persistence and developer APIs at the same layer. ## Why the launch wave arrived during a hardware shortage

In late September, TrendForce projected **10–15% quarter-on-quarter growth in conventional DRAM contract prices** for Q4 2026 and **15–20% for NAND flash**. Its October research tied growing server CPU and DDR5 demand to inference and agent workloads. Reuters had already reported CPU lead times of up to six months for some Intel customers in China in February. Those are supply constraints and price forecasts, with different scopes. [September memory forecast](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260930-13258.html) · [October CPU and DDR5 research](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20261029-13284.html) · [Reuters on CPU lead times](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/intel-amd-notify-customers-china-lengthy-waits-cpus-2026-02-06/)

Most of these boxes run Git, browsers and builds on ordinary CPUs. Inference can happen elsewhere, with its accelerator cost on the model bill. Packing bursty jobs onto shared servers and moving idle state to disk can free enough CPU and RAM for the next customer. Agents that revisit repositories or handle incoming events give providers a reason to package that lifecycle separately from the model.

## How long can the cheap plans last?

Agent37’s founder says the company earns healthy margins and reports a free-tier infrastructure cost of **$0.05–$0.10 per user per month**. In the same discussion, he attributes that figure to a cap of **about 10 awake hours per week** and says the host starts restarting memory-heavy sandboxes when RAM pressure becomes critical. Those are useful disclosures about the current workload and policy. The company hasn’t published enough cost or load data to tell whether the price holds if many customers run browser-heavy agents or builds at the same time. [Agent37 launch discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50059096)

InstaBox described the same capacity problem from the other side. It says automated free-tier abuse exhausted memory and took down nodes. Its free plan now limits users to one machine and pauses it after 30 minutes of inactivity. Manus offers a different precedent. In October, UsagePricing documented the removal of its **$10 Basic** computer, leaving **$30** as the entry price for new buyers. The two cases show how an offer can change through usage limits or through the plans available at checkout. [InstaBox pricing and abuse account](https://instabox.sh/blog/instabox-pricing) · [Manus pricing history](https://www.usagepricing.com/blueprint/activity/manus-2026-10-15-cloud-computer-basic-tier-dropped)

Suppliers can change the economics too. Hetzner raised prices for new orders and resizes in June, citing hardware procurement costs, while keeping existing contracts on their terms. A startup could therefore have cheaper capacity today than it can rent for its next batch of customers. Competition can pull the other way. In October, Fly.io cut its modeled price for a fully utilized 2-vCPU, 4 GB Sprite from **$226.80 to $118.08** over 720 hours. Agent37’s supplier rates and future pricing remain unknown. [Hetzner’s June adjustment](https://www.hetzner.com/pressroom/standardization-and-price-adjustment-of-our-server-products/) · [Fly.io’s October update](https://fly.io/pricing-update/)

For the overnight build, a cheap sandbox earns its keep if it delivers the patch by morning. For a bot or a workspace your customers rely on, the better test is what happens when several agents wake together, whether a restart loses work, and whether you can move the files and state if the price changes. The launch price is easy to verify. Those are the questions worth answering before you depend on it.
